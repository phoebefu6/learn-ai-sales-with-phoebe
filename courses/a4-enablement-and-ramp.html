Enablement, coaching and ramp

Every call your team runs is now recorded, transcribed and scored by default. That single fact is either the best coaching input a sales organisation has ever had, or the fastest way to lose the trust of your strongest reps - and which one it becomes has almost nothing to do with the tool you bought. It is decided by three org-design choices you will make in the next month: who sees the transcript first, whether a score can be used against someone without a human looking at it, and where consent is captured. Tonight also rebuilds ramp, because a searchable record of real calls changes what a new rep can learn in week one.

Part 1 · the choice that decides everything else

Coaching, not surveillance 10 min live

Ten years ago, coaching a call meant a manager sitting in on it, or a rep describing it afterwards from memory. Now the transcript, the talk ratio, the question count, the sentiment curve and an automated score all exist before the rep has left the meeting. Nothing about that data is inherently good or bad. What matters is the loop you build around it, and there are only two loops. Your team will work out which one they are in within about three weeks, and they will be right.

Three commitments separate them, and each one is a decision you make rather than a setting you configure:

  • Coaching insights go to the rep first. The person on the call sees their own transcript, their own score and their own patterns before any manager or dashboard does. This costs you nothing and changes everything about how the data feels.
  • Scores are never used punitively without a human review. No automated rating triggers a performance conversation, a ranking or a comp consequence on its own. A human listens to the call first. Write this down, because the first time it is tested is the time it counts.
  • Consent is captured before the recording starts. Not in a calendar footer, not in a terms link, not implied by the fact that the tool joined. A person says it out loud, at the top of the call, and the other side gets a real chance to decline.
LiveWhat surveillance actually costs you, in order4 min▶

Leaders rarely choose the surveillance loop deliberately. They choose a dashboard, and the loop arrives with it. The damage sequence is predictable enough that you can watch for it:

Week 1 - the dashboard goes up. Talk ratio, question count, filler words, a composite score. It looks like insight and it is genuinely interesting to look at.

Week 3 - someone gets ranked in a meeting. No malice involved. A leader shares the board because the numbers were right there.

Week 5 - reps start performing for the metric. Talk ratio is easy to game: ask more questions you do not care about and stay quiet while the buyer says nothing useful.

Week 8 - the difficult calls stop being recorded. A rep with a wobbling deal takes it to their mobile. The exact conversation you most needed to coach is now invisible.

Week 12 - your best rep updates their CV. Not over the dashboard itself. Over what it told them about how they are seen.

The coaching loop costs one behavioural commitment and gets you the opposite: reps who bring you the wobbling deal on purpose, because they have learned the recording is for them.

Real world

The tell is where the data lands first. Ask any vendor to show you the rep's own view of their own calls, not the manager's dashboard. If the rep view is thin, an afterthought, or does not exist, you are being sold a surveillance product with coaching language on the box. That is a purchasing decision you can make in ten minutes, and it will shape your culture for years.

LiveThe consent script, written out3 min▶

Consent is the part everyone agrees with in principle and quietly skips in practice, because saying it out loud feels awkward for about four seconds. Give your reps the words and the awkwardness disappears. This is the whole script:

Say this before the recording starts 
"Before we get going - I use a notetaker that records and transcribes
this call so I can stay in the conversation instead of typing. It stays
in our system, I'll send you the recap afterwards, and I'm happy to turn
it off if you'd rather. Is that okay with you?"

Then: wait for an actual answer. Then start the recording.
What most teams do instead 
A line in the calendar invite footer: "Meetings may be recorded for
quality purposes." The bot joins, announces itself in the chat, and
nobody says anything.
  • Before, not during. Consent captured after the recording starts is not consent, it is a notification. This is the rule the source map carries into leader session a5 as well.
  • Check your jurisdictions, not just your head office. Recording consent varies by country and by one-party versus all-party rules. If your team sells across borders the script has to satisfy the strictest jurisdiction on the call.
  • Give a real exit. "Happy to turn it off" has to be true. A rep who has never once turned it off is probably not offering it properly.
  • This is orientation, not legal advice. Take your script to counsel with a list of the jurisdictions you sell into. Session a5 covers the full compliance rails.
Self-studyCall scoring rubrics that measure behaviour, not personality3 min read▶

Most automated call scores drift toward personality traits dressed as metrics, and personality is not coachable in a one-to-one. A usable rubric scores things a rep can change on the next call by deciding to.

Score this (behaviour)Not this (personality)Why it matters
Did they ask about the buyer's timeline and get a date?"Confidence" or "energy"One is an act with a yes or no answer. The other is a judgement about a person
Did a second stakeholder get named and a route to them agreed?"Rapport" or "likeability"Multithreading is a repeatable move; being liked is not a skill you can assign as homework
Was the next step agreed with a date and an owner before the call ended?"Enthusiasm" or "passion"The single strongest predictor of advance, and it is entirely under the rep's control
Did they let a silence sit after asking a hard question?"Talk ratio" as a headline numberTalk ratio is a proxy that gets gamed within a fortnight. The underlying behaviour is the thing

Two further rails. Scores are inputs to a conversation, never outputs of a judgement - the rubric tells you what to listen for in the call, it does not tell you the answer. And every rubric item must be demonstrable in a real recorded call from your own team, otherwise you are coaching a theory.

Part 2 · the new-hire question

Ramp on a searchable record 9 min live

New-rep ramp used to mean shadowing whoever happened to be free that week. The new hire got four or five calls, chosen by availability rather than by relevance, and formed their entire model of how you sell from a sample of five. With a real record of calls, recaps and objections, a new rep can study forty real discovery calls in their first week - and study them properly: paused, re-read, searched by objection, compared across reps. That is a genuinely different starting position, and it is the strongest enablement argument for any of this technology.

The research points the same way, and it is worth being precise about what it says. Gartner found that organisations emphasising upskilling sellers on AI are 2.4x more likely to see good revenue growth, and that those providing AI-enabled next best actions are 2.6x more likely to achieve commercial growth. Read both carefully: they are findings about enablement, not about tools. Nobody in either group got the multiple by buying software. They got it by changing what happens after the software arrives.

LiveBuilding the 10-call starter library4 min▶

Forty calls is the ceiling. Ten curated calls is the thing that actually works, because a new hire with an unfiltered archive will watch three and give up. The curation is manager work and it cannot be delegated to a search box.

Two calls that went well and are typical. Not your best call ever. The ordinary good one, so the bar looks reachable.

Two calls where a hard objection was handled honestly, including one where the rep conceded a genuine weakness and kept the deal alive.

Two calls that died, with a one-line note from you on where it went. New reps learn more from a clean loss than from three wins.

Two calls from different reps on the same segment, so the hire sees there is a range rather than a script.

One call where the buyer said no and was right. This is how you teach qualification without a slide about qualification.

One recent call on the anchor account - for our running case, Northwind Logistics: new Jurong DC, three ops reqs open. The hire learns your live territory, not a museum piece.

Pair each call with a single question to answer in writing. "At what point did the buyer decide this was worth their time?" beats "watch and take notes" by a distance, and it gives you something to talk about in the debrief.

Refresh the library every quarter Ten calls from eighteen months ago teach a market that no longer exists and a product that has changed twice. Put the refresh on the same cadence as your pricing review and it will actually happen.
LiveThe one-behaviour-per-week coaching rule3 min▶

An AI call review will hand a manager fourteen observations about a single conversation, all of them defensible. Deliver all fourteen and the rep changes nothing, because fourteen simultaneous corrections is not feedback, it is a verdict on their competence.

  • One behaviour. One week. Chosen together. The rep should be able to say what they are working on without checking their notes. If they cannot, you picked too many things.
  • It must be observable in a recording. "Be more consultative" cannot be checked. "Ask for the timeline before talking about price" can be, on Thursday, in call three.
  • Measure it on their calls, and show them. This is where the tooling genuinely earns its money: you can count the behaviour across ten calls instead of arguing about impressions.
  • Then stop. When the behaviour holds for two weeks, close it and pick the next one. Leaving old items open turns coaching into an ever-growing list of ways the rep is failing.

Four behaviours a month, thirty-six a year, each one actually landed. Compare that with the fourteen-point review that changes nothing, and the arithmetic makes itself.

Self-studyWhat not to automate in coaching2 min read▶

Three things belong to a human manager permanently, and each one gets quietly outsourced the moment a tool offers to draft it:

  • The difficult conversation. Underperformance, a broken commitment, a behaviour that is hurting the team. An AI-drafted version of this is legible as AI-drafted within one sentence, and the rep will remember for years that you could not be bothered to write it yourself.
  • The performance plan. It has consequences for someone's livelihood, so a person has to own every line of it. This is the same rail as session 3's committed number: a promise, and a threat, both stay human.
  • The praise. Most overlooked of the three. Automated recognition is worth less than none, because it teaches the team that your attention can be faked. Specific praise, said by name, about a thing you actually noticed, is the cheapest management tool that exists and AI makes it cheaper to counterfeit.

Everything upstream of those three is fair game: transcription, summaries, pattern-finding, the objection library, the ramp curriculum, the search across four hundred calls that no human would ever run. Automate the preparation. Never automate the relationship.

Self-studyAn objection library built from your own calls3 min read▶

Every sales org has an objection-handling document, and almost every one of them was written by marketing, in a room, from imagination. The version built from your own recorded calls is a different artifact entirely, because it contains what buyers actually say and what actually worked.

Extract, do not invent. Search the transcript archive for the moments a buyer pushed back. Cluster them by theme and count them - you will find your real top five is not the one on the slide.

Attach the real responses, quoted. Both the ones that landed and the ones that did not. The contrast teaches faster than a recommended phrasing ever will.

Mark the ones where the honest answer is "you are right". Every product has them. A library that pretends otherwise trains reps to bluff, and rep session b6 is built around not doing that.

Re-run the extraction quarterly. Objections move with the market, the competition and your own release notes. A static library is a snapshot of a quarter you have already left.

This is one of the few enablement assets that genuinely gets better with volume, and it is the clearest answer to the question your CFO will eventually ask about what the recording tooling is actually producing.

Homework

Before session 5 ◐ 60-90 min total

  • Write your team's recording consent script in the rep's own spoken words, then check it against every jurisdiction you sell into. If the strictest one on your list needs all-party consent, the script has to satisfy that one. Take it to counsel before it goes live.
  • Pick one behaviour and coach only that, across the whole team, for two weeks. Choose something observable in a recording. Count it before you start so you have a baseline to point at.
  • Build the 10-call starter library for your next new hire, using the mix in Part 2, with one written question attached to each call.
  • Open your current call-scoring rubric and cross out every item that scores personality rather than behaviour. Whatever survives is your real rubric.
  • Answer the course question: your reps are getting close to five hours a week back. How much of it went into coaching, and how would you show that to your CRO?

Check yourself

Three questions before you go 🎯 ◐ 90 seconds

1 · Two teams use the identical call-recording product. One builds trust, the other loses its best reps. What actually differs?

The transcript is the same data in both loops. What changes the outcome is whether the rep sees their own call first, whether scores are ever used punitively without a person listening, and whether consent was taken out loud. Those are decisions, not settings, and your team will work out which loop they are in within about three weeks.

2 · Gartner finds that organisations emphasising upskilling sellers on AI are 2.4x more likely to see good revenue growth. What does that finding actually support?

Both Gartner numbers in Part 2 are enablement findings, not tool findings. The 2.6x on AI-enabled next best actions is the same shape: nobody in either group earned the multiple by purchasing software. Read them as evidence for the coaching and ramp investment you were tempted to cut.

3 · Which of these should a manager never hand to AI?

Automate the preparation, never the relationship. The difficult conversation and the performance plan carry consequences a person must own, and automated praise is worth less than none because it teaches the team your attention can be faked. Everything upstream of those three is fair game.
