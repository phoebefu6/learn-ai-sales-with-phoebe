Part 1 · covers call-recording consent

Consent first, and say it like a person 6 min live

Consent to record is captured in the opening seconds of the call, out loud, before the recording is doing anything worth keeping. It is not a line in the calendar invite, not a clause in your email footer, and not an assumption you make because the bot joined and nobody objected. Requirements vary by jurisdiction and by whether the rule is one-party or all-party consent, so this page is orientation only - check your list of countries with counsel, and leader session a5 covers the org-level policy version. What follows is the part that is yours either way: the eight seconds where you say it and mean it.

LiveThe eight-second consent line, written out4 min▶

Most reps get this wrong by making it either too small (mumbled, apologetic, half a sentence) or too big (a paragraph that sounds like it was read off a compliance card and makes the buyer wonder what she has walked into). Say this, at normal speed, before anything else:

Say this, first thing "Before we start - I use a note-taker so I can actually listen instead of typing. It records and transcribes, and I am the one who writes the summary afterwards. Are you ok with that?"

Thirty-two words. Roughly eight seconds. Three things are doing work in it:

  • You said what it does. Records and transcribes. Not "I have an assistant on the call", which is how a buyer ends up surprised later.
  • You gave the reason, and the reason is about her. So you can listen. That is true, and it is the whole argument of Part 2.
  • You asked a question and then you stopped talking. A consent line that runs straight into your first agenda item is not consent, it is an announcement.

Then wait. The pause is two seconds and it feels like ten. Let it happen.

LiveWhen she says no, and why the call is still fine3 min▶

Some buyers will decline, and a few will decline in a way that sounds like suspicion. This is not a problem to negotiate. It is a one-line answer you should have ready so the silence does not become awkward:

The answer "No problem at all - I will take notes by hand and send you the summary either way. Say if I am typing too loudly."
  • Turn the bot off in front of her. Do not just say you will. Remove it from the meeting so she can see it leave the participant list.
  • Do not argue the policy. "Most of my customers are fine with it" is pressure, and it lands as exactly that. If a regulated buyer says no, the no is often not even hers.
  • Write less, not more. Without a recording, the temptation is to transcribe by hand and stop listening. Capture five things: her words for the pain, the numbers she cites, the names she mentions, the objection she raises twice, and the next step.
  • The recap still goes out inside the hour. Your hand-written recap will be shorter and often better, because you were forced to decide what mattered while she was still talking.

Reps overestimate how often this happens and underestimate how badly it goes when they have no answer prepared. Have the line. Use it twice a quarter. Move on.

Self-studyThe three consent mistakes that survive because nobody objects3 min read▶

None of these are exotic. All three are common, and all three depend on the fact that a buyer usually will not challenge you in the first minute of a first call:

  • Footer consent. A line in the calendar invite saying meetings may be recorded. Nobody read it, and in an all-party consent jurisdiction it is not obviously worth anything. It also fails the human test: if you would not be comfortable pointing at it mid-call, it is not consent.
  • Bot-joined-so-it-is-fine. The note-taker appears in the participant list with a name, and the reasoning is that the buyer saw it. She saw a participant. She did not agree to a recording, and she certainly did not agree to it being searchable by your whole revenue org next quarter.
  • The late joiner. Jonas from IT drops in at minute twelve because Mira pulled him in. He was not there for your consent line. He starts talking about their security review. Tell him before he speaks, every time, in one sentence: "quick note, I have a note-taker recording."

Requirements genuinely differ by country and by one-party versus all-party rules, and this course does not give legal advice. Take your actual list of jurisdictions to counsel once, write down the answer, and stop improvising it per call. Leader session a5 is the org version of that conversation.

Part 2 · what the tool actually bought you

The note-taker bought you attention, now spend it 7 min live

The value of transcription is not the transcript. Almost nobody reads a 6,200-word transcript, including the person who recorded it. The value is that you can put the pen down and look at her. That is a real gain - Gartner puts AI's time saving at nearly five hours a week per seller, and 72% of sales organisations fail to reinvest that time in anything high-value. On a live call the failure mode is faster and more personal than that statistic sounds: the rep half-listens because it is recorded anyway, asks a question Mira already answered, and produces a file nobody opens. A transcript is a record. An understanding is a decision. Only one of them moves a deal.

LiveThe half-listening failure, and how you catch yourself doing it4 min▶

Nobody decides to half-listen. It arrives as relief: the pressure to capture is gone, so the attention drifts to the next question on your list, to the tab behind the call, to what you will say next. The tells are visible on the recording afterwards, which is the one genuinely good use of listening back to yourself:

  • You asked something she already answered. The most reliable signal, and the most expensive one - it tells the buyer directly that you were not there.
  • You never followed a thread. Every question came off your prep list in order. b4's question bank is a bank, not a script. If Mira says "the DC ramp is three weeks behind", the next question is about that, not about question four.
  • You have no idea what she said the number was. If a buyer cites a figure and you cannot repeat it back within thirty seconds, you were transcribing in your head instead of listening.
  • You talked more than she did. On a discovery call this is close to fatal and it is trivially measurable on any recording.

The rule that fixes all four: spend the attention out loud. Reflect back. "So the ramp is three weeks behind and the action items from the Monday ops call are the part that keeps slipping. Have I got that right?" You cannot say that sentence while half-listening, which is exactly why it works as a check.

Self-studyWhy "within the hour" is the actual rule3 min read▶

The recap window is not a productivity nicety. Three things decay fast after a call, and all three decay in the first few hours:

  • Your memory of the shape. The transcript keeps her words. It does not keep what she paused before saying, what she said flatly, or which of the three problems she came back to twice. That is the part that tells you what the deal actually is, and it is gone by tomorrow.
  • Her willingness to correct you. A recap that arrives while the call is still fresh gets read and edited. One that arrives on Thursday gets archived unread, and every wrong assumption in it silently becomes the record.
  • The next step's momentum. A next step with a date, agreed in writing on the same day, holds. The same next step raised three days later has to be re-sold.
Real world

Pacific Freight's Tuas ramp took action items closed within a week from 41% to 88% in one quarter. That is the Cadence proof point you use in outreach, and it is worth reading honestly here: the number did not move because the meetings were recorded. It moved because someone wrote down what was agreed, with an owner and a date, while the meeting was still warm. The tool made that cheap enough to do every time. It did not do it.

Part 3 · the craft

The recap, the room, and the record cards · read in any order

Five cards. The first one is the template you will use for the rest of your career, and it has four sections and no fifth. The rest are the situations that break it: a room with four people in it, a colleague typing loudly, and a searchable archive that outlives the deal.

LiveThe recap template, in full4 min▶

Four sections. Same four, every time, in this order. Under 200 words total, and it goes in the body of the email, never as an attachment.

SectionWhat goes in itWho writes it
What I heardThree to five facts she said, in her words. Numbers, dates, names. No interpretation.AI may draft, you verify every line
What we agreedThe shared conclusions. "The Jurong ramp is the priority, not the HQ rollout."AI may draft, you cut anything she did not actually agree
What I owe you, by whenYour commitments with a date on each one. This is the promise.You. Always. Never generated.
What you owe me, by whenHer commitments with a date. Usually one, often an introduction.You, and you say it plainly

Then one closing line, which is the only sentence in the recap doing emotional work: "Correct anything I got wrong - I would rather fix it now than build on it." That line is what turns a summary into a shared record, because it invites the edit that makes it hers too.

  • No product paragraph. Not one sentence. The recap is a record of her situation, not a second pitch.
  • No adjectives about the call. "Great conversation" is filler and it flags the whole email as generated.
  • Every line is a fact she said or a commitment you made. If a line is neither, cut it. That single test is tonight's build-along.
LiveWhat AI may draft, and the one thing you always write3 min▶

The trust line from b1 lands directly on this page. The read, the promise, the price stay human. A recap contains all three in miniature:

  • AI may draft "what I heard". It has the transcript and it is good at extraction. You then check every line against what she actually said, because a confident summary of something she did not say is worse than no summary.
  • AI may draft "what we agreed" - and you will cut half of it. Models are generous about agreement. They turn "that could be interesting" into "agreed to explore". That drift is how a deal ends up in your forecast on the strength of politeness.
  • You write "what I owe you". This is the promise, and it is the sentence she will hold you to. Never auto-generated, never once, not even the dates.
  • You write anything about price. If the call touched commercials, the recap either says the number you actually said or says nothing at all.

The failure mode is not that the model lies. It is that it smooths. Every rounded-off qualifier makes the deal sound one notch further along than it is, and by b9 you are forecasting on a stack of smoothed recaps.

LiveNote-taking etiquette in the room3 min▶

The bot is not the only thing capturing. You are in the room too, and how you behave with the capture is part of what she is evaluating:

  • Announce it once, then stop referring to it. Repeatedly mentioning the recording makes people careful, and careful buyers tell you less.
  • Do not read from a script. b4's question bank stays out of frame. The moment your eyes are visibly scanning a list, the conversation becomes an interview.
  • Say it when you type. "Give me one second, I want to get that number down." That is not an interruption, it is a signal that what she said mattered enough to stop for.
  • Never take a note during the hard part. When a buyer says something uncomfortable - budget frozen, a bad experience with a previous vendor - the typing sound is what stops her. Look at her. Write it after.
  • If a colleague is on the call, agree the roles first. One person leads, the other captures and asks the follow-ups the leader misses. Two people both half-leading is worse than one person alone.
LiveThe multi-stakeholder call: capture who said what4 min▶

Mira brings Jonas Weber from IT to the second call, and Priya Raman the CFO joins for the last fifteen minutes. A single-voice recap now actively damages you, because it flattens three different positions into one imaginary consensus. Attribute everything:

  • Name the speaker on every heard-line. "Mira: the Jurong ramp is three weeks behind. Jonas: any tool needs to clear the security review, roughly four weeks. Priya: nothing new gets signed before the Q3 close."
  • Attribution is not bureaucracy, it is a map. Read those three lines back and the deal shape is obvious: your timeline is set by Jonas, your budget window by Priya, and your champion is Mira, who controls neither.
  • Disagreement in the room is the most valuable thing you captured. If Jonas and Mira disagreed about the rollout order, write it down as a disagreement. Do not resolve it in the recap on their behalf. That is not your call to make, and making it is how you lose Jonas.
  • Send the recap to everyone who was on the call. Including the person who said nothing. Silence in a meeting is not agreement, and a recap is the cheapest way to find that out early.
  • A note-taker's speaker labels are not reliable enough to trust unchecked on a room with a shared microphone or two people with similar voices. If you are attributing a commitment to a named person, verify it.
Self-studyWhat your note-taker retains, and who else can search it4 min read▶

You told Mira you were recording. You almost certainly did not tell her the two things she would actually care about, because most reps do not know the answers. Find them out this week:

  • How long is it kept? Default retention on meeting-intelligence tools is often indefinite. A call from a deal you lost eighteen months ago may still be sitting there, fully transcribed and searchable, along with everything Mira said about her own leadership.
  • Who can search it? In most deployments the answer is "everyone in the workspace", which means a rep who has never met Mira can search her name and read a candid forty-five minutes. That is usually fine and occasionally very much not.
  • What happens when you leave? The record outlives you, which is the argument for it (b8 is about writing for your successor) and also the reason to be careful about what you type into a call summary.
  • Does the buyer get a copy on request? Under PDPA in Singapore and comparable regimes elsewhere, personal data brings access and correction rights. If Mira asks for what you hold on her, "I do not know" is not an answer you want to give.

Write down your org's actual answers, once, and keep them in the same note as your consent line. Then when a buyer asks the follow-up question - and a security-minded one like Jonas will - you answer in a sentence instead of promising to check. Leader session a5 covers setting these policies rather than just knowing them.

Build-along

Run one recap end to end ★ 20 min · everyone does it

You have a rough transcript of the Mira Chen call: forty-one minutes, roughly 5,800 words, speaker labels mostly right. We are going to turn it into a recap she reads, and then cut that recap until every remaining line is either a fact she said or a commitment you made. Nothing else survives. Do it in the room, timed.

Minute 0-2 · before you open the transcript, write the next step from memory, with a date. If you cannot, that is the finding: you did not close the call properly, and no summary tool fixes that.

Minute 2-6 · extract. Run the good prompt below over the transcript. You get a first draft in four sections. Do not edit it yet, just read it.

Minute 6-10 · verify "what I heard" line by line. Search the transcript for each claim. Delete anything you cannot find her saying. Most people delete one or two lines here, and one of them is usually a number.

Minute 10-14 · cut "what we agreed" hard. Anything that was interest rather than agreement comes out. "Open to a pilot" is not "agreed to a pilot". Expect to lose half of this section.

Minute 14-18 · write the two commitment sections yourself. Close the draft. Type them from scratch, each with a date. This is the promise, and it never comes out of a model.

Minute 18-20 · the survivor test. Read every line and label it F (a fact she said) or C (a commitment made). Anything unlabelled gets deleted. Count your final word count - the target is under 200.

Prompt that works - extraction with a refusal built in 
Below is a transcript of a 41-minute discovery call between me (AE at
Cadence) and Mira Chen, VP Ops at Northwind Logistics.

Produce three sections, in this order, and nothing else:

1. WHAT I HEARD - up to 5 factual statements Mira made. Use her own
   words where possible. Include every number, date and name she said.
2. WHAT WE AGREED - only conclusions BOTH of us stated. If she
   expressed interest without agreeing, put it under HEARD, not AGREED.
3. OPEN QUESTIONS - things left unresolved on the call.

Rules:
- Quote or paraphrase only what is in the transcript. If something is
  not in the transcript, do not include it.
- If you are unsure whether a line is agreement or interest, put it in
  HEARD and flag it as UNCERTAIN.
- Attribute every line to the speaker who said it.
- Do not write commitments, next steps, dates, pricing, or any
  closing paragraph. I write those myself.
- Do not add adjectives about how the call went.
Prompt that produces the email nobody replies to 
Write a follow-up email summarising our great conversation with
Mira at Northwind Logistics and suggesting next steps. Keep it
warm and professional.
Why the second prompt fails, in one line It asks for an email, not a record. So the model writes the genre it knows best - pleasantries, a smoothed summary, and an invented next step - and every one of those is a place where something Mira never said quietly becomes the thing you both now believe.
Homework

Try it yourself - this week ◐ 35 min total

  • Say the consent line out loud three times before your next call, until it stops sounding like a disclaimer. Then use it on a real call, with the pause after the question.
  • Record one real call with consent captured out loud in the opening seconds. If the buyer declines, run the hand-notes version and do the rest of this homework anyway.
  • Write the recap in under 20 minutes, using the four sections. Time yourself honestly. The first one usually takes 35 and the third one takes 12.
  • Send it within the hour, with the correction line at the bottom. Same day is not the same thing as within the hour.
  • Note what the buyer corrects, word for word. That correction is the single most valuable output of the whole session - it is the gap between what you heard and what is true, measured for free.
  • Find out your note-taker's retention period and who in your company can search it. Write both answers next to your consent line so you can answer the question live next time.
  • Answer the course question: the note-taker gave you your hands back on that call. What did you do with them?

Check yourself

Three questions before you go 🎯 ◐ 90 seconds

1 · Your calendar invite says "meetings may be recorded" and the note-taker is visible in the participant list. Is that consent?

Footer consent fails on two counts: nobody read it, and in an all-party jurisdiction it is unlikely to be worth much. It also fails the human test. Say it out loud in eight seconds, ask the question, and stop talking. And tell late joiners like Jonas before they speak, because they were not there for the line.

2 · What is the real product of a recorded call?

A transcript is a record; an understanding is a decision. Nobody reopens 6,200 words, but a 200-word recap sent while the call is still warm gets read, gets corrected, and becomes the thing both sides refer back to. Gartner's finding applies exactly here: the hours come back automatically, the value does not.

3 · Which line of a recap must never be drafted by a model?

The trust line from b1: the read, the promise and the price stay human. AI may extract what was said, and you verify it. The commitment you are making, and the date you are making it by, is the sentence the buyer will hold you to. It gets typed by you, from scratch, every time.
