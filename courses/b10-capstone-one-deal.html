Part 1 · the whole deal loop, in order

Assemble the deal 9 min live

Lay the nine artifacts out in the order they happened and read them as one document. Most reps discover the same thing when they do this: the pieces are individually fine and collectively inconsistent. The pain in the email is not the pain in the recap. The proposal promises something the call never discussed. The CRM record says "good conversation" where the recap says the champion has no budget line. Consistency across the whole thing is what a buyer experiences as competence.

Real world

The inconsistency check is the whole exercise. Read the email's pain sentence and the recap's pain sentence back to back. If they are different problems, one of them was invented - usually the email's, because it was written before anyone had spoken to her. That gap is invisible to you and completely visible to the buyer, and it is the most common reason a strong first meeting goes quiet.

Self-studyThe Northwind deal, written out4 min read▶

A worked version, compressed to one screen, so you can compare it against yours:

  • Day 0 - lease filing plus three open warehouse-ops reqs. Dated, public, checkable. Two other accounts on the list get no trigger in six minutes and go to nurture.
  • Day 1 - 71 words. Trigger, the ramp pain in her language, Pacific Freight's 41% to 88%, and one question she can answer from her phone.
  • Day 4 - she replies with one line: "it is on the list, not top of it". That is a real answer, and it sets the whole deal's urgency honestly.
  • Day 6 - the call. Consent in the first eight seconds. The hypothesis was that decisions were being lost between site meetings; she corrects it - the decisions are captured, they just never reach the night shift. Better problem, and not the one you guessed.
  • Day 6 - recap within the hour, with her correction in her words at the top.
  • Day 13 - the objection: "we already get transcripts from the video tool". Classified as authority, not price - the real blocker is that Jonas in IT owns the video tool and has not been in a single conversation.
  • Day 20 - proposal. Every line traceable. One number. Priya in finance gets the same number translated into the cost of a slow ramp, not into minutes saved.
  • Day 34 - record written for a stranger, next step dated, and a commit you would repeat in front of the team.

Note what is missing from that list: nothing clever. The deal was won by nine ordinary artifacts that agreed with each other.

Part 2 · the twelve rows

Score it honestly ★ 12 min · everyone runs it

Twelve rows: five levers, three trust-line checks, four rails. A deal does not need 12/12 to be a good deal, but you should know exactly which rows you failed and why - and any failed rail row is a stop, not a deduction. Score the Northwind deal first as a group, then your own.

#RowPasses when
1Trigger eventA dated public fact with a source, used in the first line
2Named painThe pain is in the buyer's words and survived contact with the buyer
3Proof pointOne comparable customer, one number, one time period
4Specific askAnswerable in one line from a phone
5No AI tellsUnder 80 words, zero tells, no unfilled merge fields anywhere in the thread
6The read was realA human looked at this account before anything was written
7The promise was humanEvery commitment traces to something a person said out loud
8The price was ownedA named human can defend the number to finance
9Consent capturedRecording consent spoken before the recording started
10Suppression respectedNo contact on this deal was ever a prior opt-out
11Jurisdiction checkedThe regimes touching these contacts were identified before sending
12Record inheritableA colleague could take the deal on Monday from the record alone
Rows 9 to 11 are stops, not points A deal at 11/12 that failed row 10 is not an eleven-out-of-twelve deal. It is a compliance incident that also happened to have good copy. Score the rails first and separately - if any rail fails, the deal does not get a score until it is fixed.
The final gate

The sequence, one last time ★ 8 min · explore

All five levers on, sequence average 10.1%. You have seen this exact configuration since b1. Tonight look only at the three rows that are still red at the top of the ladder, and say out loud why that is the correct outcome rather than a failure of the writing.

Jonas Weber, 1.8%. Right company, wrong seat. Nothing you write reaches a person with no pain and no budget line.

Anna Kovac, 1.2%. A perfect email to a company that cannot buy at your entry price. It costs you sending reputation and gains nothing.

Marcus Hale, 0.0%. Prior opt-out. This is the one row on the board where zero is the professional outcome, and the row that turns a scorecard into a rail.

Toggle every lever off and back on. Confirm those three never move. That is the course in one gesture: copy is stage 2, and stage 1 decided the ceiling.

Optional, for old times: switch on ⚠ Max personalisation tokens and watch 10.1% become 5.7%.

Real world

10.1% is a good week, and it is capped by decisions you made before you wrote a word. That is not a discouraging finding, it is a liberating one: the highest-leverage half hour in your week is not writing, it is deciding who is worth writing to. Every rep who has ever said "our messaging is not working" while sending to a list nobody pruned has been trying to fix stage 2 with a stage 1 problem.

Part 3 · after tonight

Week one back at your desk 5 min live

LiveThe five-hour question, answered3 min▶

Every session in this track ended by asking where the saved time went, because the research is blunt about it: AI hands sellers back nearly five hours a week, and 72% of sales organisations reinvest none of it. Sellers who genuinely partner with AI are 3.7x more likely to hit quota - the word doing the work in that sentence is "partner", not "use".

  • The default - the hours refill with more sends to the same list, more tabs, and more dashboard admin. Nobody chose this. It just happens.
  • The choice - name one thing the hours buy: six minutes of real trigger work per prospect, one more discovery call a week, or the recap that goes out within the hour instead of on Friday.
  • Write it down. An intention that is not written is the 72%.
Self-studyWhat this course deliberately did not teach2 min read▶
  • Vendor certifications. Those stay with the vendors, and they change every quarter. This track taught the working core that survives a tool swap.
  • Building or tuning models. Reps configure. Nothing here required it.
  • Legal advice. The compliance material is orientation and a checklist to take to counsel, and it says so on every page it appears.
  • RevOps architecture and CPQ. Adjacent, real, and out of scope for sixteen sessions.

If you lead a team, the six-session leader track covers what sits above all of this: the forecast, enablement, the rails and the rollout. If you are a rep, keep one habit rather than eight - the trigger habit is the one that pays.

Check yourself

Three questions before you go 🎯 ◐ 90 seconds

1 · A deal scores 11 of 12 rows, and the row it failed is "suppression respected". How should it be reported?

Rows 9 to 11 are rails. A prior opt-out that received a send is a compliance failure whatever the copy looked like, and averaging it into a score is exactly how it becomes invisible.

2 · Reading the nine artifacts in order, the pain in the email is not the pain in the recap. What does that usually mean?

The email is written before anyone has spoken to the buyer, so its pain is a hypothesis. The call is where you find out. Fix it forward: the recap and everything after it carry the real pain, in her words.

3 · At the final gate, three rows stay red with all five levers on. What is the correct conclusion?

Wrong seat, under the ICP floor, and a prior opt-out are stage-1 decisions. Copy is stage 2 and cannot lift a ceiling stage 1 set - which is why b2 comes before b3, and why "our messaging is not working" is so often a targeting problem in disguise.
